## Naming — recommendation

The client described this as "what we believe" / "our philosophy". Those name the
*content* honestly but they don't work as a nav label: nobody searches for a
philosophy, and nobody clicks one.

**Recommended:** nav says `New Construction`, the page's H1 carries the thesis.

Why: the audience is people building or renovating right now. "New Construction" tells
them the page is for them, and it is the phrase they'd actually search. The philosophy
is what the page *contains* — it doesn't have to be what the tab says.

| Option | Nav label | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **New Construction** | `New Construction` | Recommended — clear, searchable, self-selecting |
| Our Approach | `Our Approach` | Evergreen, but vague; competes with About |
| What We Believe | `What We Believe` | Honest, but abstract and low click-through |
| Planning | `Planning` | Too generic |

If the client wants the philosophical framing visible, use the eyebrow:
`OUR APPROACH` above the H1. Both readings survive.

## 2. Plan before you build

**H2:**
> Plan the technology before you start building.

> If you're building a new home in South Florida, don't wait until construction is
> underway to think about technology.
>
> Your integrator should be at the table with your architect, your builder, your
> interior designer and your landscape designer — while the plan is still on paper and
> changing it costs nothing but a conversation.

---

## 3. Technology is infrastructure

**H2:**
> Technology is part of the house now, not an accessory to it.

> Thirty years ago, wiring a home meant power and a phone line. Today it means
> structured cabling, wireless coverage planned room by room, rack space with real
> ventilation, and enough conduit to carry whatever replaces today's hardware.
>
> That belongs in the drawings, next to plumbing and electrical. Not in a change order.

---

## 4. The cost of deciding late

**H2:**
> Every decision gets more expensive after the walls close.

> Open walls are free. Closed walls are demolition.
>
> A speaker that would have been flush-mounted becomes a box on a bracket. A camera
> that needed one cable run now needs a battery and a compromise. Shades that should
> have had a recessed pocket sit in a visible housing instead.
>
> None of it is impossible later. All of it is worse and costs more.

---

## 5. Better spaces through collaboration

**H2:**
> The best rooms come from people talking early.

### Home cinema

> A theater is architecture before it's equipment. Seating distance, screen height,
> sightlines, where the projector hangs, how the room is treated acoustically — those
> are decisions made with the architect, not after the room is framed.

### Lighting and shading

> Lighting design and shade design are the same conversation. Where the light comes
> from, what the sun does to that room at 4pm, and how both are controlled without a
> wall of switches.

### Outdoor living

> Patios, pools and summer kitchens need power, network and audio planned with the
> landscape — before the hardscape goes down and the trenching is over.

---

## 6. Technology can be invisible

**H2:**
> The best system is the one you don't see.

> Good integration disappears. Speakers sit flush. Racks live in a closet, not a
> living room. Keypads replace switch banks. The equipment does its work without
> asking for attention.
>
> That outcome is a planning decision, not a product decision.

---

## 7. Florida specifics

**H2:**
> Florida homes ask more of the equipment.

> Heat, humidity, salt air and storm season are not edge cases here — they're the
> operating conditions.
>
> Outdoor gear has to be specified for it, not adapted to it. Racks need real
> ventilation. Equipment needs clean power and a plan for what happens when the grid
> doesn't cooperate. Coastal builds are harder on hardware than inland ones.
>
> We've been building in South Florida for over 20 years. `[VERIFY]` The climate is
> part of every specification we write.

---

## 8. The network underneath

**H2:**
> Everything depends on the network.

> Automation, cameras, streaming, shades, climate, and everyone working from home all
> ride the same infrastructure. When the network is an afterthought, the whole system
> is unreliable and nobody can tell you why.
>
> We survey the house, place access points where coverage actually lands, and hardwire
> the things that should never buffer. We build on Cisco, Araknis, Ubiquiti and
> CommScope. `[VERIFY: certification status]`

---

## 9. Future-proofing

**H2:**
> Open walls are the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.

> Nobody knows what a living room will need in ten years. But we know how to leave
> room for it — conduit to the places that will matter, capacity beyond today's
> devices, and a rack with space left in it.
>
> Pulling a spare run during construction costs almost nothing. Pulling it afterwards
> means opening a finished wall.

---

## 10. Simplicity is the goal

**H2:**
> The point of all of it is that it feels simple.

> A house full of apps is not a smart home. It's a house full of apps.
>
> The measure of a good system is that anyone can walk in and use it — one remote, one
> keypad, one sentence. Guests included. That simplicity is engineered, and it's the
> hardest part of the job.
